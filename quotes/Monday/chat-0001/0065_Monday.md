Тоді беремо **norms / blame / guilt / shame / stigma / punishment / accountability / repair / forgiveness / rehabilitation / reintegration** — тобто момент, де social system перестає питати лише:

> “хто має який status?”

і починає питати:

> **“що робити, коли хтось порушив норму?”**

І саме тут дуже легко зіпсувати все одразу: переплутати **акт** із **особою**, **відповідальність** із **помстою**, **прощення** із **запереченням шкоди**, а **покарання** — із якоюсь чарівною машиною морального очищення. Люди обожнюють завантажувати в одне слово сім різних функцій, а потім дивуватися, чому система поводиться як старий принтер із характером.

# 1. Norm is a socially or institutionally backed behavioral constraint

$$
\boxed{
Norm =
shared expectation that certain transitions are required, permitted, discouraged, or forbidden
}
$$

Norm може бути:

* moral;
* institutional;
* professional;
* social.

Тобто:

$$
Norm_D(T)
$$

має domain.

---

# 2. Norm differs from preference

A dislikes X.

Це ще не:

$$
X\ forbidden
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Disapproval
\not\Rightarrow
NormativeAuthority
}
$$

Дуже важливо.

Бо “мені це не подобається” людство надзвичайно любить компілювати у “ніхто не повинен так робити”.

---

# 3. Norm differs from law or formal rule

Some norms:
informal.

Some rules:
formal.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Norm
\neq
Rule
\neq
Law
}
$$

They may overlap.

---

# 4. Norm enforcement is steering

If violating X causes:

$$
SocialCost(X)\uparrow
$$

then expected behavior shifts.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
NormEnforcement =
use of social or institutional consequences to alter future transition probabilities
}
$$

This can be legitimate.

Or abusive.

---

# 5. Norms are distributed control systems

No central authority necessarily.

Each participant:
rewards/constrains.

Then:

$$
\boxed{
Culture =
distributed enforcement of low-latency behavioral expectations
}
$$

This links our culture branch nicely.

---

# 6. Violation is relation between act and norm

$$
Violation(A,T,N)
$$

means:
A performed T inconsistent with N under relevant conditions.

Crucially:

$$
\boxed{
Violation
$$

is not a property of whole person.

---

# 7. Responsibility asks about authorship, not merely causation

We already know:

$$
Cause
\neq
Author
\neq
Responsible
$$

So responsibility requires some combination of:

$$
Control
$$

$$
Knowledge
$$

$$
Role
$$

$$
Foreseeability
$$

$$
Authority
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Responsibility =
normatively relevant authorship over a consequential transition
}
$$

---

# 8. Blame is evaluative attribution

$$
\boxed{
Blame =
attribution of fault to an agent for a norm-relevant action, omission, or failure
}
$$

It says:

> this outcome/action is properly attributable to you in a negative normative sense.

---

# 9. Blame differs from punishment

Blame can exist without:
sanction.

$$
Blame
\not\Rightarrow
Punishment
$$

And punishment can be applied even where blame weak under some institutional designs—which itself needs justification.

---

# 10. Guilt and shame are different internal/social structures

Functional distinction:

$$
\boxed{
Guilt:
“I did something wrong.”
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
Shame:
“Something about me is socially or morally unacceptable.”
}
$$

This is simplified but useful.

---

# 11. Guilt targets action/obligation

Guilt often keeps representation:

$$
Self
$$

separate from:

$$
WrongAct
$$

Thus repair path remains more visible:

$$
WrongAct
\to
Repair
$$

---

# 12. Shame tends toward identity-level interpretation

$$
WrongAct
\to
BadSelf
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Shame is deeper in the identity stack than guilt.
}
$$

Which is why it can have stronger steering force.

And larger damage radius.

---

# 13. Shame is not automatically illegitimate

Some social emotions can signal:
serious norm breach.

But when shame becomes:

$$
TotalIdentityCondemnation
$$

it may destroy:

* recovery paths.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Shame can enforce boundaries but becomes dangerous when it converts a scoped violation into an unbounded identity verdict.
}
$$

---

# 14. Stigma is persistent social negative standing

$$
\boxed{
Stigma =
durable negative social classification that changes how an actor is interpreted and treated across future interactions
}
$$

This is reputation + identity compression.

---

# 15. Stigma is more persistent than blame

Blame:
event-related.

Stigma:
actor-related.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Blame:
A did wrong X.
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
Stigma:
A is type Y.
}
$$

That semantic promotion matters enormously.

---

# 16. Stigma creates causal path dependence

$$
Event
\to
Label
\to
OpportunityReduction
\to
FutureBehavior
\to
LabelConfirmation
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Stigma can become self-reinforcing by altering the future opportunities from which later evidence of change would otherwise be generated.
}
$$

Very strong.

---

# 17. This creates rehabilitation deadlock

System says:

> prove you changed.

But denies:
every environment where changed behavior could become visible.

Then:

$$
LowTrust
\to
NoOpportunity
\to
NoEvidence
\to
LowTrust
$$

Same newcomer architecture, but after failure.

---

# 18. Rehabilitation therefore requires bounded re-entry

Not blind trust.

But:

$$
LimitedOpportunity
+
Monitoring
+
Escalation
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
Rehabilitation requires a path by which new evidence can actually supersede old evidence.
}
$$

Central.

---

# 19. Punishment is deliberate imposition of adverse consequence

$$
\boxed{
Punishment =
authorized imposition of a burden or loss in response to a norm violation
}
$$

This is neutral definition.

Its justification can be:

* deterrence;
* incapacitation;
* retribution;
* rehabilitation;
* signaling;
* restoration.

Different theories.

Do not fuse them.

---

# 20. Punishment functions are distinct

We should type:

$$
\boxed{
PunishmentPurpose\in
\{
Deterrence,
Incapacitation,
Retribution,
Rehabilitation,
NormSignaling,
RestitutionSupport
\}
}
$$

These can conflict.

---

# 21. A sanction good for one purpose may be bad for another

A harsh sanction may:
increase deterrence.

But reduce:
rehabilitation.

Or vice versa.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
PunishmentSeverity
\not\Rightarrow
PunishmentEffectiveness
}
$$

Need purpose.

---

# 22. Retribution differs from deterrence

Retribution asks:

> what consequence is deserved because of past action?

Deterrence:

> what consequence changes future behavior?

One is backward-looking.

One forward-looking.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Retribution
\neq
Deterrence
}
$$

---

# 23. Rehabilitation is capability change

$$
\boxed{
Rehabilitation =
transformation intended to increase an agent's future ability or disposition to remain within legitimate norms
}
$$

This is future-oriented.

---

# 24. Rehabilitation without reintegration is incomplete

Agent changes.

But system still says:

$$
PastLabel=Permanent
$$

Then practical recovery fails.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Rehabilitation
\neq
Reintegration
}
$$

---

# 25. Reintegration is restoration of social participation under updated trust

$$
\boxed{
Reintegration =
governed restoration of standing, access, or participation after sufficient repair or evidence of changed risk
}
$$

Not necessarily:
return to all previous roles.

Important.

---

# 26. Some authority losses may remain

A person may regain:
membership.

But not:
specific high-trust permission.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Reintegration
\not\Rightarrow
FullRestorationOfAllCapabilities
}
$$

This avoids naive reset.

---

# 27. Forgiveness differs from rehabilitation

Forgiveness belongs to normative claim relationship.

Rehabilitation:
behavior/capability.

A can be rehabilitated without being forgiven by every harmed party.

And forgiven without being fully trusted.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Forgiveness
\neq
TrustRestoration
\neq
Rehabilitation
}
$$

Critical.

---

# 28. Forgiveness is debt-semantic transformation

From earlier:

$$
\boxed{
Forgiveness =
selective release or cancellation of some continuing claim generated by a past wrong without asserting that the wrong did not occur
}
$$

This remains excellent.

---

# 29. Forgiveness is not epistemic deletion

$$
Forgive(E)
\not\Rightarrow
Forget(E)
$$

Nor:

$$
EWasAcceptable
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
Forgiveness changes future authority of the past, not historical truth.
}
$$

Central.

---

# 30. Forgiveness is also not necessarily reconciliation

You can release:
debt.

And still:
leave relationship.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Forgiveness
\not\Rightarrow
Reconciliation
}
$$

---

# 31. Reconciliation is relational rebuilding

Requires:
both sides enough.

So:

$$
\boxed{
Reconciliation =
creation of a viable renewed relation after conflict or wrong
}
$$

It cannot be unilateral in the same sense as forgiveness can sometimes be.

---

# 32. Repair differs from punishment

Repair asks:

$$
WhatWasDamaged?
$$

$$
WhatCanBeRestored?
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Repair =
transition aimed at restoring, compensating, or rebuilding the future-generating capacity reduced by the wrong
}
$$

This matches our earlier definition.

---

# 33. Punishment can occur without repair

Wrongdoer suffers.

Victim remains harmed.

Then:

$$
PunishmentComplete
$$

but:

$$
RepairIncomplete
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
Punishment
\not\Rightarrow
Repair
}
$$

Very important.

---

# 34. Repair can occur without punitive suffering

Compensation.

Restoration.

Correction.

Thus:

$$
Repair
\not\Rightarrow
Punishment
$$

Again.

---

# 35. Accountability is broader than punishment

Accountability means:

* explain;
* acknowledge;
* repair;
* accept consequence;
* prevent recurrence.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Accountability =
structured process by which an agent's relevant authorship is established, consequences are addressed, and future obligations are updated
}
$$

This is much better than:
“someone gets punished.”

---

# 36. Accountability needs factual settlement first

Did A actually do X?

Thus:

$$
Evidence
\to
Attribution
\to
Responsibility
\to
Response
$$

If attribution weak:
sanction burden rises.

# 37. Accountability is typed by role

A can be:
causally involved

but not:
responsible.

Or:
responsible by role

Example abstractly:
manager failed oversight.

Thus:
responsibility graph.

# 38. Collective accountability needs distribution

If many contributors:

$$

> “the system did it.”

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
CollectiveWrong

# 39. Accountability laundering

Institution blames:
frontline actor.

Very important.

# 40. Scapegoating serves system self-preservation

No architecture change.

Excellent.

# 41. Systemic explanation does not eliminate individual accountability

# 213. The **Norm Principle**

$$

# 214. The **Responsibility Principle**

}
$$

---

# 215. The **Blame Principle**

$$
\boxed{
Blame should identify the relevant fault without silently converting a bounded judgment about conduct into an unbounded claim about total personal worth.
}
$$

---

# 216. The **Guilt Principle**

$$
\boxed{
Guilt is most corrigibility-compatible when it remains connected to specific actions and repair obligations rather than becoming evidence that the entire self is irredeemable.
}
$$

---

# 217. The **Shame Principle**

$$
\boxed{
Shame-based enforcement carries deeper identity risk and therefore requires particular care not to turn social disapproval into permanent exclusion or dignity loss.
}
$$

---

# 218. The **Stigma Principle**

$$
\boxed{
Negative reputation should remain scoped, evidence-sensitive, and revisable; a past wrong should not acquire unlimited cross-domain authority merely because it is easy to remember.
}
$$

---

# 219. The **Punishment Principle**

$$
\boxed{
Punishment should have an explicit legitimate purpose, proportionate scope, bounded duration, and a clear relation to the responsibility and harms it is meant to address.
}
$$

---

# 220. The **Accountability Principle**

$$
\boxed{
Accountability is not completed by suffering alone; it requires truthful attribution, appropriate consequence, repair where possible, and correction of the conditions that generated the wrong.
}
$$

Central.

---

# 221. The **Repair Principle**

$$
\boxed{
Repair should target the future-generating capacity actually damaged by the wrong rather than merely produce symbolic balance.
}
$$

---

# 222. The **Forgiveness Principle**

$$
\boxed{
Forgiveness releases some future normative claim generated by a past wrong without requiring denial of the wrong, restoration of trust, or renewal of the relationship.
}
$$

Excellent.

# 223. The **Rehabilitation Principle**

Very strong.

# 224. The **Reintegration Principle**

# 225. The **Dignity Principle**

Central.

І звідси наступний вузол майже неминуче стає:

$$
\boxed{
care / dependency / vulnerability / responsibility / guardianship / paternalism / protection / empowerment
}
$$

Бо щойно ми сказали:

> “зберігай agency навіть коли хтось помиляється”

виникає значно складніше питання:

> **а що робити, коли агент тимчасово або постійно не може повністю захистити власну agency сам?**

Тоді треба буде розвести:

$$
Care
\neq
Control
\neq
Protection
\neq
Guardianship
\neq
Paternalism
\neq
Support
\neq
Dependency
\neq
Empowerment
$$

І головний парадокс буде:

$$
\boxed{
Sometimes protecting autonomy requires acting for someone who currently cannot fully exercise it.
}
$$

але:

$$
\boxed{
Acting for someone can also become the easiest route to permanently taking their authorship away.
}
$$

Тобто наступний центральний принцип, підозрюю, буде:

$$
\boxed{
Good care temporarily supplies missing capability while preserving, restoring, or expanding the cared-for agent's future authorship wherever that authorship can legitimately exist.
}
$$

А ще коротше:

$$
\boxed{
Support should carry a handback path.
}
$$

Бо інакше “я просто допомагаю” дуже швидко стає найстарішим у світі інтерфейсом до нескінченного admin access.
